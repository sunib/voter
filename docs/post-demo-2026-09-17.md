# What broke in front of two hundred people

A post-mortem of the Voter demo on **2026-09-17**, written four days later from
the cluster's own records. Three defects, two of which nobody reported at the
time and one of which several people did, in words that turned out to be almost
exactly right.

The short version: we shipped a controller that writes to an object, and a
validator that refuses writes if that object has been written to. They went out
in the same commit. Then dark mode hid the error message that would have
explained it.

---

## The setup

Voter is a conference demo. The audience scans a QR code, enrols through Room
Pass, and ends up with a real Kubernetes identity — their own OIDC token, their
own RBAC grants. Everything they then do on their phone is a real API call made
as them. They vote in a quiz round (a `QuizSubmission` per person), they order
coffee, and at the end the presenter widens their permissions and the whole room
gets to edit the coffee menu (`CoffeeConfig`) at once, with every save arriving
in Git as a commit authored by whoever clicked.

The point of the demo is that none of this is a mock. The refusals are real
refusals. Which is exactly why a real 409 is indistinguishable from a bug.

The running image was `sha-b8beaca`, deliberately not the newest `main` — a
sensible call before a talk, and not a factor in any of what follows. All three
defects are in `b8beaca`, and two of them are still in `main`.

Timeline, from the objects and the pod log:

| Time (UTC) | What |
|---|---|
| 09:43:48 – 09:48:36 | 67 distinct participants sign in |
| 09:45:27 – 09:48:36 | Voting on round `demo1`. **38 ballots recorded** |
| 09:58:42 – 09:59:56 | ~60 coffee orders |
| 10:00:13 – 10:06:20 | The room edits the coffee menu. **26 saves succeed** |
| 10:13:35 | Round `evaluation`, 2 ballots |

## Defect 1: a subset of the room could not vote

### The symptom

Most people voted. Some pressed Submit and were told:

> The round changed. Reload the questions before voting.

Reloading worked. Which is why the demo survived, and why the arrivals in the
table above are spread across three full minutes rather than the twenty-second
burst you would expect from two hundred phones.

### The mechanism

A round is a `QuizSession`. When a phone opens the answer screen it does one GET
and keeps the object, including its `metadata.resourceVersion`. When the person
submits, the browser sends that value back, and the backend checks it:

```go
// voter/participant_quiz.go
if req.ResourceVersion == "" || req.ResourceVersion != round.GetResourceVersion() {
    writeJSON(w, 409, map[string]string{"error": "The round changed. Reload the questions before voting."})
    return
}
```

The intent is good and the rule is one every reviewer would wave through: *do not
accept answers to questions that have since changed.* Swapping the questions
under someone mid-answer is the thing the round rules forbid, and this is the
guard that enforces it.

The problem is what `resourceVersion` actually means.

In the same release we added a controller that makes the live tally a field on
the round. It watches `QuizSubmission` objects and patches
`QuizSession.status` — the status subresource, specifically, so that it can
publish a result without being able to edit the round. The commit message was
proud of this, and it was right to be:

> `metadata.generation` now means something again — it is what stops a status
> write from triggering the next one, which would otherwise be an unbounded write
> loop with nothing wrong on screen.

That sentence contains the whole bug, and we did not hear it.

**A status-subresource write does not move `metadata.generation`. It does move
`metadata.resourceVersion`.** That is the entire point of the split: `generation`
tracks spec intent, `resourceVersion` tracks *the object's storage revision*, and
every write to any part of the object bumps it, status included. The controller
was built on that distinction. The vote handler was built on the wrong half of
it.

So: every time anybody's ballot landed, the controller recomputed the tally and
patched the round's status, and every phone still looking at the answer screen
was instantly holding a `resourceVersion` that no longer existed.

### The proof

The controller coalesces bursts into one write per second, and resyncs every
round every sixty seconds regardless of activity — a deliberate heartbeat, so
that a stopped timestamp on the projector means a stopped controller.

Four days after the talk, on an idle cluster, with nobody voting and nothing
happening:

```
08:30:05   demo1  resourceVersion=5279679   lastTallyTime=08:30:05Z
08:31:49   demo1  resourceVersion=5279994   lastTallyTime=08:31:05Z
```

The round is closed. No ballots exist that did not exist four days ago. Its
`resourceVersion` changed anyway, because `lastTallyTime` moves every sixty
seconds and a changed timestamp is a real write.

**At rest, a ballot was valid for at most sixty seconds after the questions
loaded. During the voting burst, it was valid for about one second.**

Demo day, round `demo1` took ballots continuously from 09:45:27 to 09:48:36.
Three minutes of arrivals, every one of them triggering a status write about a
second later. Essentially every phone that sat on the answer screen for longer
than it took someone else to vote was refused.

67 people signed in before voting closed. 38 ballots exist.

### Why only a subset

Because the failure is a race with a moving target, not a broken code path.

You got through if your load-to-submit interval happened to fall in a gap
between two status writes — if you were quick, or if the room went briefly
quiet, or if you reloaded (which is what the message told you to do, and what
enough people did). You did not get through if you read the questions carefully,
or if you were still deciding while somebody else voted.

Which is a nasty property for a demo: **it selects against the people paying the
most attention.**

## Defect 2: "it says the CRD is not the newest version"

### The symptom

During the coffee-menu segment, several people reported an error on save about
the version not being the newest. That is a remarkably accurate description of
something we never wrote.

### The mechanism

There is one `CoffeeConfig` object. At this point in the demo the presenter has
granted the whole room permission to edit it. So sixty-odd people are editing a
single object, and every save carries a `resourceVersion` precondition, because
conditional writes are the correct thing to do and the demo is partly *about*
doing the correct thing.

When two of those saves race, the loser gets the API server's own words, which
the backend passes through verbatim so that a refusal in this demo is always
Kubernetes' refusal and never a paraphrase:

```
Operation cannot be fulfilled on coffeeconfigs.coffee.configbutler.ai "demo-coffee":
the object has been modified; please apply your changes to the latest version and
try again
```

Read that as somebody in row fifteen holding a phone: a long dotted name that
looks like a CRD, and the words *latest version*. "The CRD is not the newest
version" is not a misunderstanding. It is a fair summary.

### The proof

Every successful save creates a `CommitRequest`, and only after the patch
succeeds — so the surviving objects are a list of the winners:

```
coffee-save-8gp9s  10:00:13
coffee-save-bbvpm  10:02:21    coffee-save-ddn4m  10:02:21
coffee-save-2757n  10:02:27    coffee-save-rpc22  10:02:27
coffee-save-l5dnd  10:03:27    coffee-save-6ndrk  10:03:27
...
coffee-save-bhp47  10:06:20
```

26 saves in six minutes, including three pairs landing in the same second on the
same object. Contention was not theoretical; it was the normal condition for the
whole segment.

### What should have happened, and nearly did

The browser already catches a 409 and re-reads the object. If the person's edits
do not overlap what changed underneath them, the editor reconciles cleanly and
tells them their edits are intact. That is good design and it mostly worked.

It falls over in two places:

1. **If the edits *do* overlap**, the editor raises per-field conflicts and
   disables Save until they are resolved. The button stops working and the
   explanation is in field markers the person has to notice.
2. **If the re-read itself fails**, the raw API server string is shown.

So the room saw a mixture: some people saw a helpful notice, some saw Save
quietly stop working, and some saw the sentence about the latest version.

### What we could not reconstruct

We cannot tell how many. The backend logs every coffee order and every login,
and **not a single refusal**. Both of these defects had to be reconstructed from
`creationTimestamp`s on surviving objects, four days later.

That is its own finding, and arguably the most expensive one. An application
whose entire premise is *the refusal is real and it is the interesting part* did
not write down a single refusal.

## Defect 3: dark mode

Nobody filed this as a bug. Several people said afterwards that they could not
read some of the buttons.

### The mechanism

The app uses PrimeVue for form controls and its own CSS for everything else.
PrimeVue 4 defaults to `darkModeSelector: 'system'` — so the moment a phone is in
dark mode, every PrimeVue component flips to a dark palette, whether or not the
application asked for one. We never passed any theme options, so we got that
default without deciding on it.

The application's own colour tokens flip correctly too. There is a
`prefers-color-scheme: dark` block, and it redefines `--bg`, `--card`, `--ink`
and the rest.

And then, below that block, 1,700 lines of stylesheet that never heard about it.
The whole file contains dark overrides for exactly two things: the token block
and the body background. Everything else that hardcoded a colour stayed light
while the text on top of it turned near-white.

### What that did

| Element | Dark-mode contrast |
|---|---|
| Multiple-choice answer rows on the voting screen | **1.18 : 1** |
| Every text input and select in the editor | **1.26 : 1** |
| Helper text throughout the voting screen | **1.25 : 1** |
| The error panel | **1.06 : 1** |
| Dirty-field and conflict-field markers | invisible |
| The change summary and recent-changes list | invisible |

WCAG AA asks for 4.5 : 1. A ratio of 1.06 : 1 is not "hard to read"; it is
white text on a white background that happens to have a faint pink tint.

The worst of these is the answer-option rows: a hardcoded `bg-white` with a
label that inherits the near-white ink colour. **On a dark-mode phone, the voting
buttons were blank rectangles.**

The second worst is subtler. The error panel is `.panel--danger`, a pale pink
background with no colour of its own, so its text inherits `--ink`. In dark mode
that is near-white on near-white — 1.06 : 1. **The component whose only job is to
explain what went wrong was the least readable thing on the page.**

## How they compound

Take one participant, in dark mode, during the coffee segment.

They edit a price. Someone else saves first. Their save returns 409. The editor
re-reads, finds a genuine overlap, raises a field conflict and disables Save.
The conflict markers that would explain this are rendered in near-white on
near-white. The error panel, if it fires, is also near-white on near-white. The
input they are looking at is near-white on near-white.

From their seat: **the Save button stopped working and the app said nothing.**

Each of the three defects is survivable alone. Together they produce a person who
cannot act and cannot find out why, and who has no reason to suspect their phone's
appearance setting has anything to do with it.

## What these have in common

They are not three unrelated mistakes. They are three instances of the same one:
**a correct local decision that stopped being correct when something else
changed, with nothing in place to notice.**

- The vote check was right until we added a writer to the object it was reading.
  Nothing failed. No test broke. The two halves were in the same commit and the
  commit message explained the exact distinction it then got wrong.
- The conditional write was right, and is still right. What was missing is that
  "everyone edits one object" turns a rare correctness guard into the common path,
  and nobody re-read the error text through the eyes of someone who would now see
  it constantly.
- The stylesheet was right when it was written, in a codebase with no dark mode.
  Adding six lines of dark tokens silently redefined the contract for every
  hardcoded colour already in the file.

The unifying gap is **nothing observed the failures**. No refusal was logged, no
test ran in dark mode, and the only signal was a person in a room putting their
hand up — which, for two of the three, nobody did.

## What we are changing

In priority order.

**1. Vote on `generation`, not `resourceVersion`.** `generation` moves only when
the spec changes, which is exactly the question the guard is trying to ask. The
tally controller already relies on this property for its own loop prevention;
the vote handler will now rely on it too. The guard keeps doing its job — a round
whose questions were edited still refuses stale answers — and a status write
costs nobody their ballot.

**2. Make a save conflict recover by itself.** On 409, re-read, and if the
person's edits do not overlap what changed, re-send automatically. Only a genuine
field-level overlap should require a human. And when one does, say so in our own
words rather than the API server's: *"Somebody else saved a moment ago. Your
edits are kept — press Save again."* The real refusals that the demo is *about*
— the 403 when you are not allowed — stay verbatim. A concurrency collision is
not that kind of refusal and should never have been dressed as one.

**3. Fix dark mode properly.** Every hardcoded surface becomes a token with a
dark counterpart. Then a CI check that fails on a hardcoded `text-black/`,
`bg-white`, or literal light `rgba(255, …)` under the frontend source, because
the fix is worthless if the next component reintroduces it.

**4. Log every refusal.** One line per refused vote and refused save, with the
subject and the reason. If this had existed, this post-mortem would have taken
ten minutes instead of an afternoon of reading timestamps.

**5. Rehearse the three things that would have caught all of this.** A phone in
dark mode. Two phones loading a round where one votes and the other waits thirty
seconds. Two browsers saving the menu in the same second. None of these take
longer than a minute, and between them they find every defect in this document.

---

## The one-line lesson

We built a demo whose argument is *"Kubernetes is the source of truth, and what
you see is really the API."* Both correctness defects came from reading the API's
own metadata slightly wrong — `resourceVersion` where we meant `generation`, and
a conflict message meant for a controller shown to a human.

Being genuinely built on the API means inheriting its semantics exactly, not
approximately. The demo was right about the hard part and wrong about the field
names.
